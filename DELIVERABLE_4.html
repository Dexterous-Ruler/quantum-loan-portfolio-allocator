2  The scaling wall, and the price of fairness

Statevector memory is 2n×16 bytes: 16 qubits fits comfortably, 28 = 4.3 GB, 30 = 17.2 GB (dead on a 16 GB laptop). The binding constraint is the budget inequality, which forces integer slack and binary expansion. — Two soft penalties — sector-concentration risk (the Markowitz term) and approval-rate parity — are squared linear forms, so both cost zero extra qubits and together they are what make this objective genuinely quadratic rather than a linear knapsack in a QUBO costume. Concentration couples applicants in the SAME sector; parity couples applicants in OPPOSITE groups.

4  Bottom line

QAOA loses on speed by ~125×, and on quality it does not cleanly win either: pooled approximation ratio 0.9789 against the greedy heuristic's 0.9986, though it reaches the exact optimum more often (67% at QAOA p=2 vs 88%) at the cost of a worse tail (worst QAOA run 0.9083 vs greedy's 0.9889). This is a negative result and that is the point — it is trustworthy precisely because it did not come out the way we wanted. We claim no advantage at 14 qubits — Guerreschi & Matsuura (Sci. Rep. 9, 6903, 2019; doi:10.1038/s41598-019-43176-9) put the QAOA crossover for combinatorial problems at hundreds of qubits, and the brief's scope is explicitly small qubit counts on a simulator. What we demonstrate is a correct end-to-end mapping — calibrated ML output → QUBO → Ising Hamiltonian → variational circuit → measured portfolio — plus an honest measurement of where it breaks. One thing QAOA gives that exact search structurally cannot: a distribution over portfolios. When the default probabilities are themselves estimates, a ranked set of near-optimal feasible portfolios beats a single optimum computed for point estimates that are wrong.
